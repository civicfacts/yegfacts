Five people under one Facebook post, in the days after Edmonton's
Infrastructure Committee met on the bike-lane program on August 26, 2026,
made five claims about how the City clears snow. Three said the City plows
its bike lanes, one of them that it does so before the streets. Another
said Whyte Avenue gets very narrow and dangerous in winter for want of
snow removal, and that the plows windrow the snow, pushing it into a ridge
at the side of the road, right into the few parking spaces left beside
the bike lanes. And a fifth, answering someone who expected the City to
ask for more snow money, said administration had two snow removal
proposals before council, both needing more spending, and that council
would soon debate bringing back calcium chloride on the roads.

The records we could find answer only the claim about the two proposals.
This page explains why the other four were set aside without a verdict,
and then reports what the records show about the proposals.

### Why four of the claims have no answer

Before the reviewers started, we looked for a record that could test each
claim. For three of them we found none. The fourth predicts a future
debate, and this site does not grade predictions.

**Bike lanes before streets.** The City's [snow and ice
procedure](/evidence/YF-EV-0281), approved in October 2025, sets a
completion standard for each kind of road and pathway. The winter
priority bike network, made up of protected bike lanes and some painted
lanes and shared paths, is to be at bare pavement within 24 hours after
a snowfall ends. So are some freeways, main roads and business
districts. Residential streets are to be brought to a five-centimetre
snowpack within ten days after a residential plowing cycle starts, and a
painted bike lane outside the priority network gets the same service as
the road it is on. Those are deadlines, not a record of which crews
cleared first after any snowfall, and we identified no published record
of that. A reader can compare the deadlines. This page draws no
conclusion from them. That claim is
[parked](/claims/bike-lanes-plowed-before-streets) until such a record is
found. The City's annual snow report, attached to the report described
below, is the first place to look, and we have not read it.

**Whyte Avenue in winter.** The same procedure lists driving lanes
narrowed below 3.2 metres by windrowed snow as one of several factors in
deciding when the City removes snow from business districts. We found no
City record giving winter lane widths or snow removal dates on 82 Avenue
through Old Strathcona, or comparing collisions there by season, so
there is nothing to measure "very narrow and dangerous" against. That
claim is [parked](/claims/whyte-avenue-narrow-in-winter) until such a
record turns up.

**Windrows in the parking.** The City's [project
page](/evidence/YF-EV-0241) lists, route by route, the parking changes
each new bike route made or was expected to make. We found no City
record of where the snow plowed from those streets, or cleared from
their bike lanes, ends up next to the parking that is left. A general
rule about windrows would not show that one filled a particular space,
and would not disprove anyone who saw it happen. That claim is
[parked](/claims/snow-windrowed-into-parking-spaces) until the City
publishes where that snow goes.

**A calcium chloride debate.** That council would soon debate bringing
back calcium chloride is a prediction. Here is what the record shows. In
March 2026 Council asked administration to include options in its snow
policy review, "including but not limited to calcium chloride (CaCl)
application to roadways" ([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0291)). The
committee agenda for August 31, published by August 22, listed an
attachment titled Summary of Calcium Chloride Usage, which we have not
read. Among its optional extras, the report lists a calcium chloride
system upgrade, with equipment and staffing to use it on major roads, at
$2.5 million a year and $4.4 million one-time
([report](/evidence/YF-EV-0294)). None of the four motions the committee
took on the snow item on August 31 names calcium chloride
([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0289)), and Council approved the new policy on
September 8 without debate. The October 2025 procedure says calcium
chloride is used to pre-wet the sand and salt mix spread on roads, and as
an anti-icer or de-icer only on top-priority pathways and public
amenities, not on bridges ([procedure](/evidence/YF-EV-0281)). That claim
is [parked](/claims/calcium-chloride-debate-coming) until a record shows
a council body debated or decided it.

Three of the four come from people arguing against the bike lanes, and
the fourth from neither side. Each comes back if the record it needs
turns up.

### What the record shows about the proposals

The comment said administration "has already put 2 different proposals
to council for snow removal and of course both will require spending
increases", and this page reads "put to council" as listed on a
published agenda of City Council or one of its committees. That reading,
and the others below, were fixed before the research. A proposal is
administration's own report or package proposing a change to snow
service. It counts as put to council once a published agenda lists it,
whether or not the meeting has happened. And it needs more spending if
its own text states a new cost. On that reading the reviewers identified
three such proposals on published agendas by August 27, 2026, the day
the comment was written. A stricter reading of the cost, or of "two",
changes the answer, as set out [below](#when-the-answer-changes).

**An active pathway snow and sidewalk package.** On City Council's agenda
for November 24, 2025, the fall budget adjustment carried an unfunded
package called Improved Accessibility - Active Pathway Snow Removal and
Sidewalk Repair ([attachment](/evidence/YF-EV-0304),
[agenda](/evidence/YF-EV-0299)). Its text asks for $2.11 million in
one-time capital. The ongoing part is $1.60 million a year for snow and
ice work in socially vulnerable areas, $1.64 million for enforcement and
$4.59 million for sidewalk repair. Its table
puts the 2026 total at $9.938 million, which the text does not reconcile
with those figures. In August 2025 a committee motion had asked
administration to prepare an unfunded package for one of the options it
had costed ([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0298)), and administration updated
the amounts for the fall package.

**A towing package for parking bans.** The same attachment asked for
$100,000 a year for contracted towing during parking bans, so that some
cars left on the street could be moved while the plows blade the roads.
Its text says it "improves service levels for snow and ice control". It
asks for ongoing money after a committee motion in June 2025 had funded
towing once, from 2025 contingency money.

**The Well Maintained City package.** The Community and Public Services
Committee's agenda for August 31, 2026 listed administration's snow and
ice policy review, report CO03513, with its attachments
([agenda](/evidence/YF-EV-0287)). An Internet Archive copy of that agenda
lists the same report and attachments ([archived
copy](/evidence/YF-EV-0288)). It is dated August 22 by the timestamp in
the archive's address for it, which our fetch report records. The page
itself carries no date. We read that copy ourselves. The reviewers
could not open it, and relied on our description of it. The report
describes a proposed package of about $23.6 million a year plus $11.5
million one-time, with an eight-day residential plowing window and a
crew for the priority bike lanes and pathways, among other changes
([report](/evidence/YF-EV-0294), [summary](/evidence/YF-EV-0295)). It
also includes turf care, street sweeping, graffiti removal and structure
inspections, and the report does not say how much of it is for snow.
Administration presented it "for information ahead of formal
presentation during 2027-2030 budget deliberations". A separate list of
24 optional extras, each with its own cost, sits beside it
([list](/evidence/YF-EV-0296)).

**How many.** The three reviewers counted differently at first: one
counted two, one three and one four. The one that counted four had
treated the list of optional extras as a proposal of its own. The one
that counted two had missed the towing package. After reading each
other's work, all three counted three. Of those three, the towing
package was the one whose place in the count was contested. Its own text
says it improves snow service, and it sits with the City's Community
Standards branch rather than its road crews. Without it, two proposals
remain, so the answer does not change on the reading above. The
reviewers could not search every meeting, so the total could be higher.

### When the answer changes

The answer holds under the reading fixed before the research. It changes
under other readings we set the reviewers to test before the research, so neither side of
the argument can fairly quote it without these.

- **On an agenda, or already dealt with.** On the rule that a proposal is
  put to council once it is on a published agenda, all three proposals
  count by August 27. On the stricter rule that a council body must
  already have dealt with it at a meeting, the Well Maintained City
  package drops out, because the committee first dealt with it on August
  31. Council dealt with the fall budget items, which held both 2025
  packages, on November 25, 2025 ([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0300)), so
  two still count on that rule, but only with the towing package. Without
  it, the claim would be only partly right. Counting City Council alone,
  and not its committees, gives the same two.
- **More money than what.** Every counted proposal states new spending.
  None states the approved snow budget it would add to. If a proposal had
  to show its increase over that budget, none would count, and the claim
  would be wrong. That says nothing about whether new money was asked
  for. An August 2025 administration report said "The total budget for
  SNIC is $67 million/year" ([report](/evidence/YF-EV-0308)), where SNIC
  means snow and ice control.
- **Two, or at least two.** We read "2 different proposals" as at least
  two. The reviewers identified three, so if the comment meant exactly
  two, it is only partly right.

### What council did with them

Putting a proposal to council is not council approving it. Report
FCS03158 says unfunded packages "would require an approved Council
amendment to add to the budget" ([report](/evidence/YF-EV-0303)). At its
budget meeting of December 1 to 4, 2025, City Council approved the fall
changes to the 2026 operating budget, 11 to 2. None of the budget
amendments in the [minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0302) names either snow
package.

On August 31, 2026, the committee recommended the new snow and ice policy
to Council, 5 to 0, on a motion by Councillor E. Rutherford
([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0289)). Mayor A. Knack moved that
administration bring the Well Maintained City package to Council on
September 8, then withdrew the motion. Two motions by Councillors J.
Morgan and J. Wright, each carried 5 to 0, asked administration to
prepare unfunded packages for the 2027-2030 budget, one to continue
assisted snow removal and one for more school zone clearing. On September
8, Council approved the policy, 12 to 0, without debate
([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0290)). The report says "no additional budget is
required" to approve the policy, and that administration will bring the
package to Council "for consideration during the 2027-2030 Budget
Deliberations".

### How the reviewers read the City's documents

The checks were made by a panel of three AI models, called the reviewers
here, under rules fixed before they started. The City's meeting portal
refuses their tools, so they read the reports, attachments, agendas and
minutes from this site's archived copies. The agendas and minutes were
given to them as items this site selected under a published rule. The
144-page budget attachment was given to them as the 4 of its 36 sections
a published rule picked as bearing on snow. All three read the same
copies, so their agreement on what those documents say is not three
separate retrievals. A label beside each of those sources says so. The
Internet Archive copy of the August agenda was not given to them, and it
carries no label.

The reviewers read the meetings named before the research. They could not
search every Council and committee meeting from November 2025 to August
2026 for other snow proposals, because the portal refused them there too.

### What this page does not answer

Nothing here says how Edmonton's crews actually clear snow, whether the
proposals should be funded, or whether taxes will rise. Winter cycling
in Edmonton is on [another page](/questions/winter-cycling), and the
bike-lane budget beside the snow budget is on [a separate
page](/questions/active-transportation).
