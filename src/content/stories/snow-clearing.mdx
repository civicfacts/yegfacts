Five people under one Facebook post, in the days after Edmonton's
Infrastructure Committee met on the bike-lane program on August 26, 2026,
made five claims about how the City clears snow. One said bike lanes get
plowed before the streets do. One said Whyte Avenue gets very narrow and
dangerous in winter for want of snow removal. One said the plows windrow
the snow, pushing it into a ridge at the side of the road, right into the
few parking spaces left beside the bike lanes. And one, answering someone
who expected the City to ask for more snow money, said administration had
two snow removal proposals before council, both needing more spending,
and that council would soon debate bringing back calcium chloride on the
roads.

The records we could find answer only the claim about the two proposals.
This page says why for the other four, and then reports what the records
show about the proposals.

### Why four of the claims have no answer

Before the reviewers started, we looked for a record that could answer
each claim either way. For four of them we found none.

**Bike lanes before streets.** The City's [snow and ice
procedure](/evidence/YF-EV-0281), approved in October 2025, sets a
deadline for each kind of road and pathway, counted from the end of a
snowfall. The winter priority bike network, made up of protected bike
lanes and some painted lanes and shared paths, is to be at bare pavement
within 24 hours. So are some freeways, main roads and business
districts. Residential streets are to be done within ten days once a
residential plowing cycle starts, and a painted bike lane outside the
priority network gets the same service as the road it is on. Those are
deadlines, not a record of which crews cleared first after any snowfall,
and we found no record of that. A reader can compare the deadlines. This
page draws no conclusion from them. That claim is
[parked](/claims/bike-lanes-plowed-before-streets) until the City
publishes a record of actual clearing by snowfall. The City's annual snow
report, attached to the report described below, is the first place to
look, and we have not read it.

**Whyte Avenue in winter.** The same procedure says the City removes snow
from business districts when windrowed snow narrows the driving lanes to
less than 3.2 metres. We found no City record of lane widths, snow removal
dates or winter collisions on 82 Avenue through Old Strathcona, so there
is nothing to measure "very narrow and dangerous" against. That claim is
[parked](/claims/whyte-avenue-narrow-in-winter) until such a record turns
up.

**Windrows in the parking.** The City's [project
page](/evidence/YF-EV-0241) says which parking each new bike route
changed. We found no City record of where the snow
plowed from those streets, or cleared from their bike lanes, ends up next
to the parking that is left. A general rule about windrows would not show
that one filled a particular space, and would not disprove anyone who saw
it happen. That claim is
[parked](/claims/snow-windrowed-into-parking-spaces) until the City
publishes where that snow goes.

**A calcium chloride debate.** That council would soon debate bringing
back calcium chloride is a prediction, and this site does not grade
predictions. Here is what the record shows. In March 2026 Council asked
administration to include options in its snow policy review, "including
but not limited to calcium chloride (CaCl) application to roadways"
([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0291)). The committee agenda for August 31,
published by August 22, listed an attachment titled Summary of Calcium
Chloride Usage, which we have not read. The report itself costs a
calcium chloride equipment upgrade for major roads at $2.5 million a year
and $4.4 million one-time, as one of its optional extras
([report](/evidence/YF-EV-0294)). None of the four motions the committee
took on the snow item on August 31 names calcium chloride
([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0289)), and Council approved the new policy on
September 8 without debate. Under the October 2025 procedure, the City
uses calcium chloride to wet the sand and salt it spreads on roads, and
as an anti-icer only on its top-priority pathways
([procedure](/evidence/YF-EV-0281)). That claim is
[parked](/claims/calcium-chloride-debate-coming) until a record shows a
council body debated or decided it.

Three of the four come from people arguing against the bike lanes, and
the fourth from neither side. Each comes back if the record it needs
turns up.

### What the record shows about the proposals

The comment said administration "has already put 2 different proposals
to council for snow removal and of course both will require spending
increases", and this page reads "put to council" as listed on a
published agenda of City Council or one of its committees. That reading,
and the others below, were fixed before the research. A proposal is
administration's own report or package proposing a change to snow
service. It counts as put to council once a published agenda lists it,
whether or not the meeting has happened. And it needs more spending if
its own text states a new cost. The reviewers found three such proposals on published agendas by
August 27, 2026, the day the comment was written.

**An active pathway snow and sidewalk package.** On City Council's agenda
for November 24, 2025, the fall budget adjustment carried an unfunded
package called Improved Accessibility - Active Pathway Snow Removal and
Sidewalk Repair ([attachment](/evidence/YF-EV-0304),
[agenda](/evidence/YF-EV-0299)). Its text asks for a $2.11 million
one-time capital investment and ongoing increases of $1.60 million a year
to clear snow first in socially vulnerable areas, $1.64 million for
enforcement and $4.59 million for sidewalk repair. Its table puts the
2026 total at $9.938 million, which the text does not reconcile with
those figures. A committee had asked for it in August 2025
([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0298)).

**A towing package for parking bans.** The same attachment asked for
$100,000 a year to hire tow trucks during parking bans, so that some cars
left on the street could be moved before the plows came through. Its text
says it "improves service levels for snow and ice control". It would
continue towing that had been paid for once from 2025 contingency money.

**The Well Maintained City package.** The Community and Public Services
Committee's agenda for August 31, 2026 listed administration's snow and
ice policy review, report CO03513, with its attachments
([agenda](/evidence/YF-EV-0287)). The Internet Archive's copy of that
agenda from August 22 lists them too ([archived
copy](/evidence/YF-EV-0288)). The report describes a package of about
$23.6 million a year plus $11.5 million one-time, with an eight-day
residential plowing window and a crew for the priority bike lanes and
pathways, among other changes ([report](/evidence/YF-EV-0294),
[summary](/evidence/YF-EV-0295)). It also pays for turf care, street
sweeping, graffiti removal and structure inspections, and the report
does not say how much of it is for snow. Administration presented it
"for information ahead of formal presentation during 2027-2030 budget
deliberations". A separate list of 24 optional extras, each with its own
cost, sits beside it ([list](/evidence/YF-EV-0296)).

**How many.** The three reviewers counted differently at first: one
counted two, one three and one four. The one that counted four had
treated the list of optional extras as a proposal of its own. The one
that counted two had missed the towing package. After reading each
other's work, all three counted three. Whether the towing package is a
snow proposal is the one judgement in the count. Its own text says it
improves snow service, and it sits with the City's Community Standards
branch rather than its road crews. Without it, two proposals remain, so
the answer does not change on the rule above.

### When the answer changes

The answer holds under the reading fixed before the research. It changes
under others the brief required the reviewers to test, so neither side of
the argument can fairly quote it without these.

- **On an agenda, or already heard.** On the rule that a proposal is put to
  council once it is on a published agenda, all three proposals count by
  August 27. On the stricter rule that a council body must already have
  dealt with it at a meeting, the Well Maintained City package drops out,
  because the committee first heard it on August 31. Council heard the
  two fall budget packages on November 25, 2025
  ([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0300)), so two still count on that rule, but
  only with the towing package. Without it, the claim would be only partly
  right. Counting City Council alone, and not its committees, gives the
  same two.
- **More money than what.** Every counted proposal states new spending.
  None states the approved snow budget it would add to. If a proposal had
  to show its increase over that budget, none would count, and the claim
  would be wrong. That says nothing about whether new money was asked
  for. The last figure administration gave, in August 2025, was "The
  total budget for SNIC is $67 million/year" ([report](/evidence/YF-EV-0308)),
  where SNIC means snow and ice control.
- **Two, or at least two.** We read "2 different proposals" as at least
  two. The reviewers' final count is three, so if the comment meant
  exactly two, it is only partly right.

### What council did with them

Putting a proposal to council is not council approving it. Report
FCS03158 says unfunded packages "would require an approved Council
amendment to add to the budget" ([report](/evidence/YF-EV-0303)). At its
budget meeting of December 1 to 4, 2025, City Council approved the fall
changes to the 2026 operating budget, 11 to 2. None of the budget
amendments in the [minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0302) names either snow
package.

On August 31, 2026, the committee recommended the new snow and ice policy
to Council, 5 to 0, on a motion by Councillor E. Rutherford
([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0289)). Mayor A. Knack moved that
administration bring the Well Maintained City package to Council on
September 8, then withdrew the motion. Two motions by Councillors J.
Morgan and J. Wright, each carried 5 to 0, asked administration to
prepare unfunded packages for the 2027-2030 budget, one to continue
assisted snow removal and one for more school zone clearing. On September
8, Council approved the policy, 12 to 0, without debate
([minutes](/evidence/YF-EV-0290)). The report says the policy itself
needs no new money and that the package will come to Council in the
2027-2030 budget.

### How the reviewers read the City's documents

The checks were made by a panel of three AI models, called the reviewers
here, under rules fixed before they started. The City's meeting portal
refuses their tools, so they read the reports, attachments, agendas and
minutes from this site's archived copies. The agendas and minutes were
given to them as items this site selected under a published rule. The
144-page budget attachment was given to them as the 4 of its 36 sections
a published rule picked, the ones that mention snow, pathways or parking
bans. All three read the same copies, so their agreement on what those
documents say is not three separate retrievals. A label beside each of
those sources says so.

The reviewers read the meetings named before the research. They could not
search every Council and committee meeting from November 2025 to August
2026 for other snow proposals, because the portal refused them there too.
Another proposal could only raise the count.

### What this page does not answer

Nothing here says how Edmonton's crews actually clear snow, whether the
proposals should be funded, or whether taxes will rise. Whether bike lanes
are worth plowing at all is on [another
page](/questions/winter-cycling), and how bike-lane money compares with
the snow budget is on [a separate page](/questions/active-transportation).
