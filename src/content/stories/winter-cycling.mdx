Every Edmonton bike-lane debate reaches the same objection, and it is not an
unreasonable one to raise: nobody bikes at -30, so why build for it here? The
forms that argument takes in circulation are set out further down this page.

### The two questions inside the objection

Two different questions hide inside that. Can cycling work as transportation
in a climate like this one? And how much cycling does Edmonton currently get?
The brief frozen before this panel ran put only the first to the reviewers,
and defined it precisely: does winter itself prevent cycling from serving a
non-trivial share of trips, *regardless of infrastructure and maintenance*.
Edmonton's current share being low does not answer that, because low uptake
has several candidate causes. What answers it is whether comparably cold,
snowy places sustain meaningful year-round cycling — which is why this story
leans on comparators rather than on Edmonton data alone.

### What Edmonton clears, and what it counts

Edmonton already treats winter cycling as an operated service rather than a
seasonal shutdown. The City assigns each active pathway a priority level and
publishes targets against them, all of which run from the end of a snowfall:

- Priority 1 cleared in 24 hours
- Priority 2 in three days
- Priority 3 in 14 days
- a connected Winter Priority Loop — three bridges, river valley trails and a
  staircase — at Priority 1

The City frames the whole thing as
best efforts, and these are stated targets rather than audited outcomes; no
reviewer found a compliance report showing how often a Priority 1 pathway is
actually clear within a day of the snow stopping.

Measurement of what riders then do is thinner. No reviewer located a recent
Edmonton household travel survey that would give a winter <Term t="mode share">mode share</Term> — a
bicycle share of all trips, on a defined winter denominator. What exists is
counter data. The City reports more than 90,000 bicycle trips counted in
December 2023, up 143 per cent on the December before, and estimates that a
quarter of Edmonton cyclists ride through the year. Counters record trips past
a sensor, not individual people, and the City publishes no note on how many
counters were running in each of the two Decembers it compares — so the size
of that jump is softer than the fact that tens of thousands of winter trips
were counted at all.

### The Oulu region's winter share

The decisive evidence comes from Finland. The 2021 national travel survey run
by Traficom — for which the Oulu region commissioned an expanded sample,
answered by more than 2,500 of its residents — found 18% of all
trips there made by bicycle — the highest share in Finland — and reported
cycling in the region as a year-round activity: in winter, residents still
averaged one trip in ten by bicycle. That is a subarctic, snowy region posting
a double-digit winter share on an all-trips denominator, which is what the
proposition under review says cannot happen. Oulu reports the network behind
it as 930 km of cycle lanes — plus 165 km of "Super" class lanes that it says
are kept in great condition around the clock. Every reviewer cautioned against
treating that share as a number Edmonton could adopt, and the reasons they
gave — decades of network building, a smaller and more compact region, shorter
journeys — are their characterizations of Oulu rather than measurements from
the cited sources. What none of them disputed is the gap all three flagged: no
source supplied a standardized Edmonton–Oulu comparison on snowfall, days of
snow cover, freeze-thaw events, or days colder than -30°C.

### Calgary's cycle tracks and Minneapolis's commute share

Two other pieces cut against the idea that cold alone decides. Calgary — the
nearest large winter city to Edmonton, sharing prairie urban form — evaluated its centre
city cycle-track pilot and found daily winter bicycle trips running, on
average, four times higher in January 2016, after the tracks went in, than in
January 2015 before them; between November 2015 and the end of March 2016 it
counted over 160,000 bicycle trips on the pilot network. And on a single
common journey-to-work denominator, the U.S. Census Bureau put Minneapolis at
a 4.1% bicycle commute share for 2008–2012, well clear of warm-winter Houston,
Phoenix, San Antonio and Dallas. Mild winters did not, by themselves, produce
cycling in those cities.

### Who keeps riding: Québec and Helsinki

None of which makes winter free. The same panel put the counter-cases on the
record. A peer-reviewed Québec study reports Montréal — Canada's strongest
cycling city — retaining 13.6% of its cyclists through the 2020–2021 winter,
and three smaller Québec cities retaining less again: cold Canadian places
where winter cycling stayed marginal.

Helsinki supplies the most uncomfortable finding of
the set. Following 384 adults in that metropolitan area over time, researchers
found residential density predicted who kept riding — travel-related urban
form only approached significance — while the length of the network and
priority winter maintenance made no significant contribution once those were
accounted for. That cuts against both sides of the argument. It undercuts "the
cold makes it impossible" — and it equally undercuts "just plow the lanes."
One reviewer drew the obvious hypothesis out: *if* Edmonton's built form is
more dispersed than Oulu's and its trips longer, that could cap what the city
reaches whatever the plows do. Nothing in the evidence measures Edmonton's
form or its trip lengths, so that stays a hypothesis rather than a finding.

So the claim as circulated is contradicted: the record affirmatively points
against cold being the binding constraint, while also showing that cold and
snow suppress cycling in every jurisdiction the panel found measurements for. What it does not
settle — and what none of the three reviewers claimed to settle — is the
number Edmontonians would actually get. No reviewer found that measured for
Edmonton.
