Every Edmonton bike-lane debate reaches the same objection, and it is not an
unreasonable one to raise. Nobody bikes at -30, the argument goes. We're a
winter city; bike lanes are for four months a year.

Two different questions hide inside that. Can cycling work as transportation
in a climate like this one? And how much cycling does Edmonton currently get?
The brief frozen before this panel ran put only the first to the reviewers,
and defined it precisely: does winter itself prevent cycling from serving a
non-trivial share of trips, *regardless of infrastructure and maintenance*.
Edmonton's current share being low does not answer that, because low uptake
has several candidate causes. What answers it is whether comparably cold,
snowy places sustain meaningful year-round cycling — which is why this story
leans on comparators rather than on Edmonton data alone.

Edmonton already treats winter cycling as an operated service rather than a
seasonal shutdown. The City assigns each active pathway a priority level and
publishes targets against them, all of which run from the end of a snowfall:
Priority 1 cleared in 24 hours, Priority 2 in three days, Priority 3 in 14
days, with a connected Winter Priority Loop — three bridges, river valley
trails and a staircase — at Priority 1. The City frames the whole thing as
best efforts, and these are stated targets rather than audited outcomes; no
reviewer found a compliance report showing how often a Priority 1 pathway is
actually clear within a day of the snow stopping.

Measurement of what riders then do is thinner. No reviewer located a recent
Edmonton household travel survey that would give a winter <Term t="mode share">mode share</Term> — a
bicycle share of all trips, on a defined winter denominator. What exists is
counter data. The City reports that more than 90,000 bike trips were counted
in December 2023, an increase of 143 per cent over the previous December, and
estimates that one in four Edmonton cyclists ride year-round. Counters record
trips past a sensor, not individual people, and the City publishes no note on
how many counters were running in each of those Decembers — so the size of
that jump is softer than the fact that tens of thousands of winter trips were
counted at all.

The decisive evidence comes from Finland. The 2021 national travel survey run
by Traficom — for which the Oulu region commissioned an expanded sample,
answered by more than 2,500 of its residents — found 18% of all
trips there made by bicycle — the highest share in Finland — and reported
that cycling in the region is year-round: even in winter, residents cycled on
average one trip in ten. That is a subarctic, snowy region posting a
double-digit winter share on an all-trips denominator, which is what the
proposition under review says cannot happen. Oulu reports its result
alongside 930 km of cycle lanes and 165 km of "Super" class lanes it says are
kept in great condition 24 hours a day. All three reviewers warned against
reading that share as a target Edmonton could adopt, and the reasons they
gave are their own characterizations of Oulu rather than measurements from
the sources: a network built up over decades, a smaller and more compact
region, shorter trips. What is not in dispute is the gap all three flagged —
no source supplied a standardized comparison of Edmonton and Oulu on
snowfall, snow-cover days, freeze-thaw events or days below -30°C.

Two other pieces cut against the idea that cold alone decides. Calgary — the
nearest large winter city to Edmonton, sharing prairie urban form — evaluated its centre
city cycle-track pilot and found, on average, four times as many daily winter
bicycle trips comparing January 2015, before the tracks, with January 2016,
after them, with over 160,000 bicycle trips counted on the pilot network
between November 2015 and the end of March 2016. And on a single common
journey-to-work denominator, the U.S. Census Bureau recorded a 4.1% bicycle
commute share in Minneapolis for 2008–2012, against 0.4% in Houston, 0.7% in
Phoenix, 0.2% in San Antonio and 0.1% in Dallas. Mild winters did not, by
themselves, produce cycling in those cities.

None of which makes winter free. The same panel put the counter-cases on the
record. A peer-reviewed Québec study reports Montréal — Canada's strongest
cycling city — retaining 13.6% of its cyclists through the 2020–2021 winter,
with 9.09% in Sherbrooke, 6.96% in Gatineau and 4.35% in Saguenay: cold
Canadian cities where winter cycling stayed marginal. And a longitudinal
study of 384 adults in the Helsinki metropolitan area found that residential
density predicted who kept riding — travel-related urban form only approached
significance — while network length and priority winter maintenance did not
contribute significantly once those were accounted for. That last result is uncomfortable for both sides of the
argument. It undercuts "the cold makes it impossible" — and it equally
undercuts "just plow the lanes." One reviewer drew the obvious hypothesis
out: *if* Edmonton's urban form is more dispersed and its trips longer than
Oulu's, that could cap what Edmonton reaches whatever the plows do. Nothing
in the evidence measures Edmonton's form or its trip lengths, so that stays a
hypothesis rather than a finding.

So the claim as circulated is contradicted: the record affirmatively points
against cold being the binding constraint, while also showing that cold and
snow suppress cycling everywhere it has been measured. What it does not
settle — and what none of the three reviewers claimed to settle — is the
number Edmontonians would actually get. No reviewer found that measured for
Edmonton.
