### The targets and the carbon budget

Edmonton City Council declared a climate emergency on August 27, 2019. The
targets the City set in its Community Energy Transition Strategy:

- cut <Term t="community emissions">community emissions</Term> 35 per cent
  below 2005 levels by 2025
- 50 per cent by 2030
- become an emissions-neutral community by 2050

A separate corporate target covers the City organization's
own operations, which are about two per cent of the total, and aims at
neutrality by 2040.

In November 2022 the City put a <Term t="carbon budget">carbon budget</Term> in front of Council
alongside the financial one. The idea is the same as a money budget, with
tonnes instead of dollars — a total the community can emit between 2022 and
2050 and still land at neutrality. That total is 176 million tonnes
<Term t="tCO2e">CO2e</Term>. The same 2022 report carried a warning in its own
summary:

> "Current investments are not enough to achieve reduction targets and action
> is needed as the carbon deficit continues to grow under the current state."

<cite>City of Edmonton, 2023-2026 Carbon Budget (FCS01478), <a href="/evidence/YF-EV-0021">YF-EV-0021</a></cite>

The City's stated cycle is a carbon budget every four years with an annual
update each fall, and those updates are where the answer to this story lives. The City measures itself, publishes the measurement, and
states whether it is on track. On the numbers the City has published, it is
not.

### The 2024 inventory against the trajectory

The most recent inventory covers 2024, and it was reported in the Fall 2025
Carbon Budget Update that went to Council on November 24, 2025. Community
emissions in 2024 were 15.2 million tonnes CO2e, a three per cent increase over
2023. The annual trajectory the City had set for itself called for 12.2 million
tonnes or less that year. The report's own words: "this 2024 target was not
met." To hit the 2025 milestone from there, emissions would have to fall to
10.8 million tonnes — 29 per cent in a single year. The report says plainly
that will not happen:

> "The trend for this year indicates this target will not be met and there are
> no current initiatives or actions that will allow the necessary reductions to
> meet the target."

<cite>City of Edmonton, Fall 2025 Carbon Budget Update (FCS03160), <a href="/evidence/YF-EV-0020">YF-EV-0020</a></cite>

The 2026 budget materials say the same thing about all three milestones:
"Edmonton's 2024 Community GHG Inventory shows that GHG emissions are not
decreasing over time and are not on track to meet the 2025, 2030 and 2050
emissions targets." And the long-run picture in the same reporting is that the
176-million-tonne allowance for the whole period to 2050 is now forecast to run
out in 2036 — a year earlier than the original 2022 forecast, not later.

### What the City has funded, proposed and measured

None of that means nothing is happening. The 2023-2026 budgets funded projects
the City estimates will cut about 180,000 tonnes over four years; there is a
Clean Energy Improvement Program now on a permanent footing, a zoning bylaw
written with climate in mind, and — as the City's own 2025 annual report notes — an A grade
maintained in its 2023 CDP climate disclosure. Per-person emissions have
genuinely fallen — 12.8 tonnes per
Edmontonian in 2024, 46 per cent below 2005. That is the strongest fact in the
"climate leader" case, and it is true.

One development lands right at this story's verification date. On August 20,
2026 the City published its "2027 Update: Climate Implementation Priorities"
package, scheduled for Executive Committee on September 2, 2026.
As of publication it is a proposal that committee has not yet considered. It
is the City's response to the gap this story documents, not a revision of the
targets, and it does not change the finding.

It is also not what the targets measure. The targets are absolute, against a
2005 baseline the City now states as 16.7 million tonnes, and against that baseline
2024 emissions are nine per cent lower. The milestone due in 2025 is 35 per
cent. Population growth is doing much of the work in the per-capita number
while the total stays roughly flat.

### The recalculated baseline and what it revised

One detail is worth stating because it cuts the other way from how these
stories usually go. In the fall of 2024 the City reported per-capita energy use
down 40 per cent — past the 2030 efficiency target in the Energy Transition
Strategy, a genuine win reported as one. Then revised natural gas data forced a
recalculation of the 2005 baseline, and the 2024 inventory showed the target had
not been reached after all; the figure is 27 per cent. The City published the
correction itself, in the same report that carries the rest of the bad news.

The same baseline revision is a reason to be careful with older numbers. CBC
reported in November 2024, from the City's Fall 2024 update, that 2023 emissions
came in at an estimated 16.2 million tonnes, against an aim of 13.4 million,
and quoted the report: "If Edmonton's current emission trajectory continues,
Edmonton's GHG reduction targets will not be achieved." That is a second
consecutive miss on the record — but those figures belong to the
pre-recalculation series and should not be subtracted from the 2024 ones. Even
the 2024 trajectory value moved: 12.7 million tonnes in the Fall 2024 telling,
12.2 million in the Fall 2025 one.

### What the record does not settle

Two things the record does not support in either direction. It does not show a
City quietly walking away from its numbers: asked directly whether the targets
would be changed given that the 2025 one is unlikely to be met, the City's
climate-plan engagement page answers that "Rather than revising the targets,
this project will focus on renewing the action plan." And it does not show a
City doing nothing. What it shows is a gap between what was adopted and what
has been measured, documented by the party with the most to lose from
documenting it.

One caveat on vintage, which matters for a claim like this. The newest
community inventory Edmonton has published covers 2024. Inventories arrive
about a year behind, in the fall carbon budget update, so 2025 — the first
milestone year itself — would not be measured in public until the fall of 2026.
Until it lands, the 2025 result is the City's forecast rather than a
measurement: its own update says the target will not be met. The inventory,
when it comes, will confirm whether that held and by how much.
