Twenty-two people argued under one Facebook post about council's
bike-lane decision over what taking road space for bike lanes has done
to Edmonton traffic. Thirteen said it causes congestion: two lanes
become one, cars back up and idle. Six said the opposite, in 11
comments, that every person on a bike is one less car in front of you. Four said the City
has taken driving lanes for bike lanes, two of them naming streets. One
said the lanes were coming out for traffic calming, with bike lanes put
in where they fit to save money. Two of them made more than one of
these claims.

The published record this site could identify cannot settle the
question they were arguing about, in either direction. This page says
why for each claim, and then reports the one thing the record can show.

### Why three of the claims have no answer

Before any research ran, the question was checked for whether a
published record exists that could answer each claim as people made it.
For three of the four, [that check](https://github.com/civicfacts/yegfacts/blob/main/reviews/lanes-and-congestion/2026-09-16/framing/check-3.md),
made on 2026-09-16 and [confirmed](https://github.com/civicfacts/yegfacts/blob/main/reviews/lanes-and-congestion/2026-09-16/framing/check-4.md) on 2026-09-23,
found none.

Whether taking a lane for a bike lane slows traffic on that street is a
question about cause. Answering it would take travel times or delays
measured on a converted street before and after the change, in a study
built to separate the bike lane from everything else that changed at the
same time. The check identified no published study of that kind for
any Edmonton street. The City did publish, in September 2025, an
[evaluation of Hermitage Road and 40 Street](/evidence/YF-EV-0173),
where the changes included bike lanes. It compares traffic volumes and
speeds from before and after, and notes some queues at intersections.
It does not measure delay in a way that could show what the bike lane
itself did to it. That claim is
[parked](/claims/lane-removal-increases-congestion) until one exists.

The opposite claim, that bike lanes ease congestion because riders leave
their cars at home, would take two things joined together: a measured
shift of trips out of cars that can be put down to the bike lanes, and
an estimate of what that shift did to delay across the city. The same
check identified no Edmonton study that does both, and that claim is
[parked](/claims/bike-infra-reduces-congestion) too.

The claim that lanes were removed for traffic calming rather than for
bikes would take a City record saying the lane would have gone even
without a bike lane, and that the two were combined to save money, as
the commenter said. The City records the check identified, including its
decision on Hermitage Road, describe the design and the traffic-calming
program but say neither of those things. That claim is
[parked](/claims/lanes-removed-for-traffic-calming) as well.

The claims set aside come from both sides. The first is the claim of
the people against the lanes, and the other two are claims of the
people for them. Each one comes back if the record it needs is
published.

Two more claims were made under this question and are not on this
page's list of checks. One, that 79 Street takes traffic that would
otherwise use 75 Street, was passed to the question about the
[Holyrood school lane](/questions/holyrood-school-lane), where it
belongs. The other, that roads carry goods, buses and emergency
vehicles, was turned down at triage as something both sides accept.

### What the record does show

The fourth claim is a fact about what the City built, and the record can
speak to it. The finding on it does not measure congestion. It says
nothing about whether traffic on any street got faster or slower.

The City's records document the 102 Avenue conversion. Before the
project, the road between 121 and 111 Street carried traffic both ways,
according to the City's [2017 fact sheet](/evidence/YF-EV-0163). The
street was too narrow to keep both travel lanes and the parking and add
a protected bike lane, so the City's recommended plan took out the
westbound lane. Its [guide to the new west-central routes](/evidence/YF-EV-0164),
issued in November 2017, confirms the change was made, with eastbound
traffic only on that stretch and the protected lane marked new that
year. The guide's map labels the neighbourhood there Oliver, which the
City's [district plans](/evidence/YF-EV-0167) place in the Central
district.

On 96 Street between 119 and 124 Avenue, in Delton, the City's
[table of its network-expansion routes](/evidence/YF-EV-0170) says the
street was made one-way southbound to accommodate a new protected bike
lane running north, listed as a 2025 route and now open for use. Parking also came
off the east side. Delton is in the North Central district.

On 100 Street in Strathcona, the same table says the street from 76 to
83 Avenue was made one-way northbound to accommodate two-way bike lanes,
and lists the stretch from 80 to 83 Avenue, also a 2025 route, as open
for use. On 110 Street in
Garneau, the City's
[final design for the neighbourhood renewal](/evidence/YF-EV-0165) calls
for removing the lane that carried southbound traffic between 76 and 82
Avenue and adding a protected bike lane along its west side. The City's
[bike route inventory](/evidence/YF-EV-0166) records that protected lane
as built in 2022, though no as-built drawing is in hand to show the
driving lane went exactly as designed. Both streets are in the Scona
district.

So the City has given up driving space for bike lanes on at least some
streets. The streets this page can document from the City's own records
are in 3 of its 15 districts.

The records do not establish the other named examples as stated. East
of 109 Street, 102 Avenue did lose a lane between 102 and 107 Street,
but the City page that records it puts the change down to the
[Valley Line West LRT](/evidence/YF-EV-0168) and does not mention a bike
lane. On 132 Avenue, the City's
[2022 answers on its final design](/evidence/YF-EV-0172) say some
stretches had four lanes, and the design leaves a lane in each direction
at minimum. Its [project page](/evidence/YF-EV-0171) records new bike
facilities finished on section after section since 2023, starting
between 101 and 107 Street. None of them pins a bike facility to
pavement that used to carry cars, so this page does not count it.

### Why that does not settle “all throughout the city”

The claim was that the City has removed lanes “all throughout the city”.
Before the research, that was held to mean a converted driving lane in
at least half the City's districts, 8 of 15, or in 5 under a looser
reading. Those bars are our judgement, not an outside standard. Three
districts is what the records in hand reach, short of either, and the
true total is unknown.

That does not make the claim false. The City's inventory of on-street
bike routes, as it stood on 2026-09-23, lists 3,175 segments and says
nothing about what any of that space was used for before. Finding out
which bike lanes replaced a driving lane means going street by street
through the City's design records, and we found no published count.
Until one exists, the records we found cannot show whether the
conversions they document are a handful or most of the city.

The three reviewers agreed on that, though not on every street, as
their [round-one answers](https://github.com/civicfacts/yegfacts/blob/main/reviews/lanes-and-congestion/2026-09-16/round1) show. All three found 102 Avenue. One also found 110 Street and a block of 83
Avenue in Strathcona. That City plan for 83 Avenue would not load when
this page was [checked](https://github.com/civicfacts/yegfacts/blob/main/reviews/lanes-and-congestion/2026-09-16/gate/freshness-audit.md), so it is not counted. Another counted 100
Street, 96 Street and 132 Avenue, working from news reports and a
community league's notice. The City's route table now backs the first
two, and 132 Avenue stays out for the reason above. The details are with the claim below.
