Twenty-four people argued under one Facebook post about council's
bike-lane decision over what taking road space for bike lanes has done
to Edmonton traffic. Thirteen said it causes congestion: two lanes
become one, cars back up and idle. Six said the opposite, that every
person on a bike is one less car in front of you. Four answered someone
who said the City had not taken any driving lanes, and named the streets
where it had. One said the lanes were going for traffic calming anyway,
with bike lanes added where they fit.

The honest answer to the question they were arguing about is that the
City's own record cannot settle it, in either direction. This page says
why for each claim, and then reports the one thing the record can show.

### Why three of the claims are parked

Before any research ran, the question was checked for whether a record
exists that could answer each claim as people made it. For three of the
four, it does not.

Whether taking a lane for a bike lane slows traffic on that street is a
question about cause. Answering it needs travel times or delays measured
on a converted street before and after the change, in a study built to
separate the bike lane from everything else that changed at the same
time. The check made before the research, as of 2026-09-23, identified
no published study of that kind for any Edmonton street. That claim is
[parked](/claims/lane-removal-increases-congestion) until one exists.

The opposite claim, that bike lanes ease congestion because riders leave
their cars at home, needs two things joined together: a measured shift
of trips out of cars that can be put down to the bike lanes, and an
estimate of what that shift did to delay across the city. The same
check identified no Edmonton study that does both, and that claim is
[parked](/claims/bike-infra-reduces-congestion) too.

The claim that lanes were removed for traffic calming rather than for
bikes needs a City record saying the lane would have gone even without
a bike lane, and that the two were combined to save money. The City
records identified before the research, including its decision on
Hermitage Road, describe the design and the traffic-calming program but
say neither of those things. That claim is
[parked](/claims/lanes-removed-for-traffic-calming) as well.

The parks fall on both sides. The first is the claim of the people
against the lanes, and the other two are claims of the people for them.
Each one reopens if the record it needs is published.

### What the record does show

The fourth claim is a fact about what the City built, and the record can
speak to it. The finding on it does not measure congestion. It says
nothing about whether traffic on any street got faster or slower.

On 102 Avenue, the commenter was right. Before the project, the road
between 121 and 111 Street carried traffic both ways, according to the
City's [2017 fact sheet](/evidence/YF-EV-0163). The street was too narrow
to keep both travel lanes and the parking and add a protected bike lane,
so the City took out the westbound lane. Its
[guide to the new west-central routes](/evidence/YF-EV-0164), issued in
November 2017, confirms the change was made, with eastbound traffic only
on that stretch and the protected lane marked new that year. The stretch
is in Oliver, which the City's [district plans](/evidence/YF-EV-0167)
place in the Central district.

On 110 Street in Garneau, the City's
[final design for the neighbourhood renewal](/evidence/YF-EV-0165) takes
out the lane that carried southbound traffic between 76 and 82 Avenue
and adds a protected bike lane along its west side. According to the City's
[bike route inventory](/evidence/YF-EV-0166), that protected lane was
built in 2022. Garneau is in the Scona district.

So a flat denial that the City has taken any driving lane for a bike
lane does not hold. Those two conversions are in 2 of the City's 15
districts.

The other named examples did not stand up as stated. East of 109
Street, 102 Avenue did lose a lane between 102 and 107 Street, but the
City gives the reason as room for the
[Valley Line West LRT](/evidence/YF-EV-0168) rather than a bike lane. On
132 Avenue, the City's [draft renewal design](/evidence/YF-EV-0169) of
early 2022 proposed a single driving lane each way with traffic calming.
A draft cannot show what was finished, or where a bike lane now sits in
what used to be a driving lane.

### Why that does not settle all throughout the city

The claim was that the City has removed lanes all throughout the city.
Before the research, that was held to mean a converted driving lane in
at least half the City's districts, 8 of 15, or in 5 under a looser
reading. Those bars are our judgement, not an outside standard. Two
districts is short of either.

That does not make the claim false. The City's inventory of on-street
bike routes, as it stood on 2026-09-23, lists 3,175 segments and says
nothing about what any of that space was used for before. Finding out
which bike lanes replaced a driving lane means going street by street
through the City's design records, and nobody has published that count.
Until someone does, the record cannot show whether the conversions it
documents are a handful or most of the city.

The three reviewers agreed on that, though not on every street. All
three found 102 Avenue. One also found 110 Street and a block of 83
Avenue in Strathcona. The City plan it cited for 83 Avenue no longer
loads, so this page does not count it. Another counted 100 Street
between 76 and 83 Avenue, 96 Street in Delton and 132 Avenue from a
community league's notice and news reports rather than a City record,
and the other two did not count them. The details are with the claim
below.
