Under a Facebook post about council's bike-lane decision, in a thread
running to 621 comments, twenty-five people argued about how much this
city rides. They did it with percentages. Two per cent of trips, said
one. Under one per cent, said another, citing the census. Eighty-seven
per cent drive, said a third. One or two per cent of the population,
said eight more. And somebody posted a counter figure of nearly 1.3
million.

Most of those numbers turn out to be right. Hardly any two of them are
about the same thing. Passages past a sensor, journeys to work, trips of
every kind, and people who ride at all are four quantities with four
denominators, and each one comes from a different instrument. That is
what the argument was: people quoting a real number at somebody quoting
a different real number. Every claim below is tested against whatever
measures that particular quantity, and each finding says what its number
is a share of.

Two of them sit at the centre. The lanes the City meters are not empty.
Cycling is also a small part of how Edmonton gets around. Both are true,
and neither answers the other.

### The counter figure, and a panel that split

Edmonton's automated counters log a bicycle whenever one goes past. Add
the City's published monthly totals across the first seven months of
2026 and you get 1,291,714, which is within a percentage point of the
figure being argued over. The commenter had not worked that out. The
sentence was reposted from a CBC News piece that analysed the same open
data in August.

So the finding is Partially supported, and the split is not about
arithmetic. Two reviewers went to the data and came back with the same
number to the unit. The third went to a developer documentation page
about the dataset instead, recorded an update date earlier than the end
of July, and could not establish from that page that the City had
published the full period.
Under a rule the brief set before anyone looked, that returns nothing:
days still to arrive can only push a total up, so a subtotal cannot
carry a verdict that depends on where the total lands.

We then ordered the claim re-run, blind, on all three seats, on the
assumption that the third reviewer had simply skipped the source. It
came back unchanged. Given the frozen brief a second time, with nothing
in the package hinting at why, that seat went to the documentation page
again and stopped in the same place. The expectation was wrong and the
mechanism caught it, which is more useful than the confirmation we were
looking for. Nobody on the panel disputes the number. What divides them
is whether the record could be shown to reach the end of July.

Two cautions belong beside it, and only one is ours. The City's own
label for what it publishes is counts rather than trips, and it warns
that the figures are raw and unassured. Ours is the arithmetic of what a
passage is: ride to work and home past one sensor and you are two, take
a single journey past two sensors and you are also two, and ride
anywhere unmetered and you are none. So the count cannot be read as a
headcount, and nothing published says how many people it represents. CBC
made that point in the article itself.

### The lanes described as empty

Ten people offered something they had seen. What they were saying could
not be checked as a report of their own perception, because no record
establishes what a passer-by noticed. It was checked instead as a claim about traffic, which the
City's meters do measure.

Five membership tests were fixed in advance, so that three reviewers
working from the same files would arrive at one list of counters rather
than three. Keep the ones configured for bicycles. Keep the ones running
on the first of July 2025. Sort each into on-street or off-street by
joining its published coordinates to the nearest built segment of the
City's route map, which carries a field with exactly two values for
that. Remove any device that quit inside the measured month. Require
twenty of the month's thirty-one days. Twenty counters survived, and
every one of them reported all thirty-one.

Their medians that month begin at 46 a day and reach 979. The bar for
"little or no" was set at 25, roughly a bicycle every forty minutes over
a sixteen-hour day, and far above anything the people making the claim
described. Nothing clears downward. At the alternative bar of 50, which
the brief also required, one of the twenty drops below and the answer
would be a qualified one instead. Neither number is borrowed from any
standard, so the finding travels with the bar it was measured against.

The streets people named do come out at the quiet end. Both meters on
132 Avenue and the one on 119 Avenue sit in double and low triple
figures while 83 Avenue runs near a thousand, an order of magnitude
between them, and that gap is real even though none of them approaches
the threshold. Whyte Avenue is not metered at all. The City's only
published bicycle counts anywhere on 82 Avenue are four manual
observations from two days in September 2016, and it says those old
counts should not be set against the automated ones. That dataset runs
from 2009 to 2016 and holds nothing at all for 119 Avenue, 132 Avenue or
Hermitage.

One device left the reckoning, and it is named here because a set that
lost a member has to say which one. The meter on 106 Street north of
Jasper Avenue, sitting on a protected lane, went silent on 2025-07-16
and never resumed. A sensor that stops is a fact about the sensor, not
about the street it was watching, which is why the brief takes it out
instead of voiding the answer. It had been running at 177.5 a day
through June, several times over the threshold, so its removal does not
help the claim.

Two boundaries on all of this. It reaches those twenty counters and no
further. They sit where the City chose to put them, and nothing found in
the search behind this page would license reading them as a sample of
the whole network. Twenty counters is also thirteen corridors, several
streets carrying a pair. And July was
picked on purpose, to test the claim in the season its holders named.
The seventeen counters in the set that have January records run from 6
bicycles a day to 212.

### Trips, commuters and drivers

Three of the circulating percentages are shares, and all three hold up.

The bicycle share of weekday travel comes from the region's household
travel survey, whose most recent published round was fielded in the
autumn of 2015 and reported in 2018. About twenty-one thousand
households kept a one-day diary, and bicycles came out at 1.7 per cent
for people living in the city. Eleven years is a long time for a number
to stand as current, and the replacement survey launched in 2025 had
released nothing by the time this brief froze. Geography moves it as well: taken across
the wider region, the same survey gives 0.8 per cent, less than half. The commenter who posted the trip share also
pointed at a Statistics Canada commuting table, which counts journeys to
work and therefore cannot produce a share of all travel, so that
citation does not carry the figure even though another document does.

The two commuting figures come out of one census table and one week in
May 2021. Bicycles were 0.88 per cent of Edmonton's commuters; cars,
trucks and vans were 85.11 per cent. Because they are two rows of the
same table they are not two independent results, and this page counts
them as one document read twice. The 87 per cent one commenter quoted is
not the city's number at all. It is almost exactly the figure for the
metropolitan area, which reaches out to St Albert, Sherwood Park, Spruce
Grove and Leduc.

That census week needs its qualification here rather than further down,
because a reader who meets those two shares without it will take away
the wrong impression. It was not an ordinary week of commuting. Working
from home in Edmonton had gone from about one employed resident in
twenty to better than one in five, and everybody working from home is
outside the commuting universe entirely. Transit's share roughly halved
between the two censuses while driving climbed by more than six points,
and bicycle commuting fell from 1.20 per cent. Widen the denominator to
all employed residents and the driving figure drops to about two thirds.
None of that shifts the findings, which are measured among people who
actually commuted, and the brief said as much before the numbers were
seen.

### The number that does not exist

Eight people quoted a share of the population who ride. Nothing
published measures it. That is the result here, and it is not a
technicality: it is the thing those eight people could not have looked
up even if they had tried.

The search behind that, as it stood on 2026-09-03, went like this.
Statistics Canada's full catalogue of released tables was pulled and
filtered, and holds no cycling participation rate for this city or its
metropolitan area. The census measures how people reach work. The 2015
travel survey measures trips inside a single weekday. The successor
survey had published nothing. The national health survey does put a
suitable question to respondents, but nothing released from it reports
an answer for Edmonton. That bounds the absence to the published record
on that date. It is not a claim that the number could never be produced.
A custom tabulation of the health survey might settle it, where its
sample is large enough for a city of this size.

There is one local instrument that asks Edmontonians how often they
ride, and it is a self-selected panel from the summer of 2014. Roughly
three in five of its respondents reported riding at some frequency,
which is not remotely one or two per cent, and that is exactly why it
cannot settle the question. Its respondents chose themselves into it, so
it cannot estimate the population in either direction.

The seasonal version fails on the same gap. To test the sentence about
one per cent riding through the winter and under fifteen per cent the
rest of the year, you need one representative sample split across the
seasons, and there is not one. The 2014 panel does ask both questions of
the same people, and nearly nine in ten of them said they never ride in
winter, but the sample is no better here than it is above. Two things a
reader might reach for are not substitutes either. The share of a year's
counter volume that falls in December, January and February is under
five per cent, and that is traffic rather than people. And the City's
own line that a quarter of cyclists ride all year divides by cyclists,
not by residents, which is a different denominator again.

One further gap sits under both. Several of the captured wordings are
not about riding at all but about using the lanes: "bike lanes for the
.001% of the population", "500 people using the bike lanes 4 months of
the year", "only 1% of the pop use them year round". Nothing published
counts the distinct residents who use a bike lane, in any season. Those
wordings were made and they are recorded here, and no riding figure is
put against them either way, because that would be answering a question
nobody asked.

### What is not answered here

Eight claims went to the panel; seven carry findings. The eighth, that
the people riding are recreational rather than commuters, does not, and
the fault is ours rather than the panel's.

The one Edmonton source that records why a trip was made is the 2015
travel survey, and its fieldwork ran on weekdays from the middle of
September to the middle of December. Both of those restrictions push
against the recreational reading, and the same brief had fixed July for
the corridor claim precisely so that a claim gets tested where it is
strongest for the people making it. A reviewer said so in a blind
re-run, we agreed, and there was no repair to make. Narrowing the claim
to autumn weekdays would answer something easier than what two
commenters actually said, and no Edmonton survey records trip purpose
across a representative stretch of the year. So it is parked in public,
with its reason and the condition that would reopen it, and its wording
is <a href="#not-checked" data-crossref>listed below among the claims
carrying no finding</a>.

A ninth never reached the panel. Somebody wrote that more than five
people rode to Heritage Days within an hour of the Saturday opening,
answering a joke made at the top of the thread. The search behind the
brief found no public count of bicycle arrivals for that event and that
hour, so it could not be settled in either direction, and it was dropped
while the brief was being written rather than sent out.

Nothing on this page says whether the lanes were worth building, whether
the money went to the right place, what any of it did to congestion, or
whether riders use the lane or the road beside it. Those are registered
separately. What is here is the arithmetic underneath the argument, with
every number's denominator attached to it.
