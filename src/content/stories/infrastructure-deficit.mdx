Under a Facebook post about a City committee's bike-lane decision in late
August 2026, the people arguing against the bike money kept making one argument
in different words. The roads are a mess. The alleys need attention. The
City says it has a huge infrastructure deficit, and it still found $100
million for bike lanes.

That argument has three parts a record can test. Are the roads in poor
shape on the City's own ratings? Was the $100 million money that could
have gone to fixing roads? And was it big enough to matter beside what the
City says it is short? Each is checked here against the City's own
documents, and each gets its own finding. A yes on one is not a yes on
another. Money can be big enough to matter without having been free to
spend, and free to spend without being big enough to matter.

### What the City's ratings say about the roads

The City grades what it owns from A, very good, to F, very poor. Its
latest published ratings are in the 2025 Infrastructure State and
Condition Report, built from data the City gathered up to December 31,
2024. Everything in this section describes the roads on that date. It cannot say what they were like when people were arguing
about them in August 2026, or whether they have changed since.

On that date the City's Roads class was worth about $10.5 billion to
replace. By that value, 11.2 per cent of it was rated poor or very poor,
70.8 per cent good or very good, 16.2 per cent fair and 1.7 per cent not
rated. The City weights its assets by replacement value, so an asset that
would cost more to replace counts for more. The report gives no share by
length.

The class is the City's own, and it holds more than driving surfaces.
On that date paved roads were 12.5 per cent poor or very
poor. Curbs, which the City files under Roads, were 7.0 per cent. Unpaved
roads had no rating at all.
This page uses the class as the City publishes it rather than building a
narrower one, and paved roads alone would give the same result.

So the finding is Partially supported. A real part of the network was
rated poor at the end of 2024. The claim that the roads as a whole were
in poor condition goes further than the ratings do. The brief drew its
lines in advance, a quarter rated poor for yes and a tenth for no, and
took neither from a published standard. At 11.2 per cent at the end of 2024, the roads landed just
above the tenth.

The appendix prints the previous report's figure beside each row, and
for Roads it is 11.4 per cent. The two are not a like-for-like trend.
The report says its own method changed, and the earlier comparison has no
separate curbs row. An older
budget, for 2019 to 2022, says Council aimed to keep under a tenth of
arterial roads in poor condition. That aim covers only the main roads,
and nobody on the panel showed it still applies.

Alleys get no finding, because nothing found gives a current citywide
rating of alley condition, and the word alley does not appear in the
2025 report. The City has described its alleys before. Its 2019-2022
budget said two-thirds of its 1,180 kilometres of alleys were in poor
condition, and it set up a program to renew alleys rated very poor. That
estimate is years old, carries no grades or date of assessment, and
cannot say what alleys are like now.

### Where the $100 million came from, and how the panel split

The money is capital profile CM-20-0330, Active Transportation
Implementation Acceleration, in the City's 2023-2026 Capital Budget. It
pays for planning, designing and building the bike network, with
wayfinding and bike parking. Its profile sheet lists one funding source
for the whole $100 million, tax-supported debt, which is City borrowing
whose servicing costs fall on the operating budget and so on taxes. The
December 2022 minutes record council approving it 9 to 4 "with funding
coming from Tax-Supported Debt". The profile lists no grant, and no
reviewer found any rule that tied that borrowing to bike lanes when
council approved it. The brief treated general City capital money of
this kind as available for road or alley renewal unless a record
restricted it.

The panel split on this claim along vendor lines. What divided the seats
was which documents they could open. The Anthropic seat, Claude Opus
5.5, got no text out of the City's budget file, and the council meeting
portal turned it away. All it had were news and advocacy pieces saying
the program ran on tax-supported debt, and the brief bars news coverage
from supplying a figure that decides a finding. It therefore treated all
$100 million as undetermined and returned Not established, while writing
that confirmation from the profile sheet would almost certainly make the
answer yes. The two OpenAI seats, GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna, had the
profile sheet and the minutes in front of them and returned Supported.

Sol and Luna come from one vendor and cannot be treated as independent,
so their agreement is one side of the split, not a majority. The site's
fixed rule turns that combination into Partially supported. Once the
panel had finished, this site checked both documents in the copies it
archived, and they match the OpenAI seats' account. That check is ours,
and the finding stays where the rule put it. Neither does the Anthropic
seat's second answer, in which it found another City record listing the
program as tax-supported debt and moved to supported, because the second
round documents errors and cannot change a finding.

The same records show what happened to the money afterwards. The profile
sheet classes the whole program as growth rather than renewal, though it
says it includes funding to top up renewal projects where the work lines
up. In December 2022, four amendments proposing other amounts for the
program were withdrawn before the $100 million carried. A December 2024
motion to strip $33.65 million from each of 2025 and 2026 lost 4 to 7.
In its Spring 2025 budget adjustment council shifted $430,000 from the bike program to a Dunluce
neighbourhood and alley renewal profile, which left it at $99.57 million,
the amount the City still reported at the end of 2025. In the records
they could reach, none of the three reviewers found money moved the other
way, out of a renewal budget and into bike lanes. In April 2023 council
passed Bylaw 20394, 8 to 4, authorizing the City to finance the project.
Two reviewers read it as tying the debt to this project, so that only a
council decision could have moved it after that. The bylaw's own text
could not be archived for this page.

### The money beside the shortfall

The City publishes its own figure for the renewal it needs and has not
funded. When council adopted the 2023-2026 budget, the City said it had a
"renewal funding gap of $1.63 billion for 2023-2026", having funded 54.4
per cent of its ideal renewal. Its 2024 annual report later put the
shortfall for the same four years at approximately $1.52 billion. Renewal
means work on things the City already owns, and the shortfall is for its
whole renewal program, not roads alone.

The $100 million is 6.6 per cent of the later figure, about a fifteenth.
Against the adopted budget's figure it is 6.1 per cent. Each comparison
puts a capital approval beside the City's own estimate of renewal it had
not funded over the same four years, and both come out the same way. The brief set a quarter of the shortfall as a
serious share and a twentieth as too small to count. The finding is
Partially supported, because the money is a real fraction of the
shortfall and well short of a serious one. Only a shortfall of $400
million or under, or above $2 billion, would have changed that.

Two more numbers put it in proportion. The program was 1.39 per cent of
the whole adopted capital budget for 2023 to 2026. And the part of the
renewal program with no dedicated money behind it got about 31 per cent
of the ideal when the budget was adopted, by the City's count, and 35.4
per cent in the later report. The City's newest annual report, for 2025,
looks ahead instead: it puts the shortfall for the next four years, 2027
to 2030, at approximately $2.7 billion.

### What this page does not answer

Drainage is in the question's wording, but this page does not check it,
and nothing here says whether bike money could or could not have gone to
drainage. <a href="/questions/snow-clearing" data-crossref>Snow
clearing</a> is a separate question on this site, and grass cutting is
not checked here. Three more claims from the same thread, about a west-end recreation
centre, the cost of new streetlights and drainage damage to homes, were
not put to the panel. They are <a href="#not-checked" data-crossref>listed
below among the claims with no finding</a>.

Nothing here says what council would have done without the program,
whether it should have chosen roads, or whether bike lanes are worth
building. Two earlier comparisons of the same $100 million, with snow
clearing and with road budgets, are on <a
href="/questions/active-transportation" data-crossref>a separate
page</a>, off the findings board for reasons given there. This page does
not replace them.
