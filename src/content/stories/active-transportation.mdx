Since council's budget vote in December 2022, "$100 million" and "bike
lanes" have travelled together in Edmonton's argument about its streets,
and the figure is almost never stated on its own. Opponents set it beside
what the City spends clearing snow. Supporters set it beside what the City
spends on roads. This story tests one comparison from each side, in the
words each was captured using, against the City's own budget documents.

### What the $100 million is

The number is real and it is a program, not a rate. In the 2023 to 2026
capital budget, council created a <Term t="capital profile">capital profile</Term> called Active
Transportation Implementation Acceleration, numbered CM-20-0330, to build out bike routes
in the older parts of the city inside the Anthony Henday. The vote split
nine to four. The money is scheduled unevenly: a small first year for
design, then three larger years. The City's own project page describes it
as about 2 per cent of roughly $5 billion in transportation projects.

Two things about that program matter for every comparison that follows.
It is a capital approval, which is permission to spend, not money out the
door. And it has been slow: by the end of 2025 the City had spent
$40.0 million of it, with most of that in 2025, as the routes that were
easiest to build went in first and the harder ones waited for design and,
lately, for council to decide what to do with them.

### The snow comparison

The campaign page that made the comparison, captured by the Wayback
Machine on 2022-12-12 during the budget debate, gives no snow figure of its
own. The <Term t="frozen brief">brief</Term> fixed one before any reviewer
looked: the Snow and Ice Control program's printed 2022 budget in the City's
approved operating budget, the annual figure in force the day the page was
captured. Against that cell, $100 million is about one and a half times a
year of snow clearing. That is a lot more than a year of snow clearing. It
is not almost double, and all three model reviewers classified the claim
the same way on that reading.

The reading matters, and the brief required the panel to say so. Winter
2022 was expensive. Council added to the snow budget three times during
that year, all one-time, for a total of $21.3 million, and against the
budget as topped up the $100 million is only 1.17 times a year of snow.
The City's own year-end table gives a third figure, an adjusted budget of
$79.5 million, which the panel could not reconcile exactly with the
adopted cell plus the additions. On the following year's budget, the
$60.9 million the City quoted for 2023, the ratio climbs back to 1.64.
None of these readings reaches double. Which side of "well above" they fall
on depends on which snow budget you hold up, so the finding is recorded as
definition-sensitive, and the verdict word travels with the name of the
budget it was measured against.

There is also the mismatch the comparison is built on. One side is four
years of capital; the other is one year of operating money. The panel
tested the claim as it was made, because that is what was said. Like for
like, one year of the program is a fraction of one year of snow clearing:
under a tenth for the first year, under two fifths on the program's
average year. And the snow
budget did not stay still: the City spent more on snow and ice control
than it budgeted in 2022, 2024 and 2025.

### The roads comparison

The councillor's comparison has a slip in it. Global News quoted him
saying roads get "$1.8 billion, 180 times as much" as bike lanes;
$1.8 billion is eighteen times $100 million, and no other record of the
remark exists, since council minutes record motions and votes rather than
debate. The brief tested the two dollar figures and the ratio they imply,
which is the substance of the point, and reported the slip as a fact about
the quotation.

The City publishes no roads-only total. What it publishes is a combined
line in the adopted capital budget, "Active Pathways and Roads Service",
which at $1.79 billion is almost certainly where the councillor's figure
came from, and which contains the bike program itself. So the brief fixed
a roads-only boundary in advance, using the City's own label on every
capital profile sheet: the profiles the City marks as Roads or as
Neighbourhood Renewal, plus three vehicle bridges whose sheets carry no
label, minus a pedestrian bridge and a parking-technology line. All three
seats summed the same profiles to the same total, $1.95 billion, about
nineteen times the bike program. That is more than the City's combined
line, because the label catches road work the City's summary table files
elsewhere, chiefly the Yellowhead Trail freeway conversion. Strip the
Yellowhead profiles and it is about $1.45 billion; keep only the profiles
literally labelled Roads and it is $1.12 billion, the one reading that
falls short of the stated figure.

The councillor's own web page states the same comparison a different way,
calling the $100 million "half a percent" of the $1.8 billion. It is
5.6 per cent. On the whole $7.2 billion capital budget the program is
1.4 per cent.

### What this does not settle

Neither claim says whether the program is worth it, and neither verdict
does. The comparison people most want checked, whether the lanes have
made traffic better or worse, is not here: the register lists it as
parked, because no published Edmonton evaluation joins the expansion to
before-and-after travel times with any basis for attributing the change
to the lanes, and it reopens when the City publishes one. Whether the lanes are used is a separate claim with its
own brief to come. What this story settles is the arithmetic both sides
have been carrying into the argument: the $100 million is a four-year
approval that is well above a year of snow clearing and nowhere near
double it, roads get an order of magnitude more, and a little under half
of the bike money had been spent by the end of 2025.
