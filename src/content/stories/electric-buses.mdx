Edmonton made its battery-electric bet in the late 2010s. In 2018, the federal,
provincial and municipal governments announced joint funding of more than
C$43 million for the first tranche. The City ordered 40 buses from <Term t="Proterra">Proterra</Term>, a U.S. electric-bus manufacturer,
later expanded the order to 60, and
publicly announced them with up to 350 km of range on ETS routes when the
first entered service in August 2020.

The fleet's troubles became public in stages. Transit-union representatives
described mechanical issues, battery problems and missing parts. In August
2023, Proterra filed for <Term t="Chapter 11">Chapter 11</Term> bankruptcy in the
United States. Edmonton's contract was not among those transferred to the
buyer of Proterra's transit business, which left the City expecting its warranty provisions would not be honoured
and without a reliable parts channel for a fleet it had paid US$58,761,600
for.

In October 2023 the City went to the bankruptcy court with an itemized
filing: it alleged substantially shorter range than contracted, and put its
actual costs to that point at C$1,352,655.58 — repair labour, parts, battery
blankets and an outstanding receivable.
The specific numbers came in February 2024, when the City filed a
<Term t="proof of claim">proof of claim</Term> seeking more than $82 million for breach of contract and negligence:
the buses were contracted to deliver 328 km of range in normal conditions
and 268 km in extreme cold, but managed roughly 165 km on average in winter —
38% short of the cold-weather guarantee — and at best about 250 km in warmer
weather,
short of even the 350 km the City had announced at launch. That proof of
claim matches the "$82 million" number that circulates on social media.

Later developments bear directly on the money question.

- **January 2024** — Proterra's transit business was sold to Phoenix Motor,
  which said it planned to restock spare parts.
- **March 2024** — Proterra's reorganization plan took effect.
- **May 2026** — the claims register still lists Edmonton's claim as
  <Term t="general unsecured">general unsecured</Term>; no allowed or paid
  amount has been published.

The City's own project page still lists all 60 electric buses in the ETS
fleet, operating from two garages.

The three claims this story checks — that the buses
failed, that Edmonton lost $82 million, and that this proves electric buses
can't work in cold cities — turn out to deserve three different answers.
