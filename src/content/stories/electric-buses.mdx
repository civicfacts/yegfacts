Edmonton made its battery-electric bet in the late 2010s. In 2018, the federal,
provincial and municipal governments announced more than C$43 million in
joint funding for the first tranche. The City ordered 40 Proterra buses,
later expanded to 60, and
publicly announced them with up to 350 km of range on ETS routes when the
first entered service in August 2020.

The fleet's troubles became public in stages. Transit-union representatives
described mechanical issues, battery problems and missing parts. In August
2023, Proterra — the manufacturer — filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in the
United States. Edmonton's contract was not among those transferred to the
buyer of Proterra's transit business, which left the City without warranty
coverage or a reliable parts channel for a fleet it had paid US$58,761,600
for.

In October 2023 the City went to the bankruptcy court with an itemized
filing: it alleged substantially shorter range than contracted, and itemized
C$1,352,655.58 in repair labour, parts, battery blankets and an outstanding
receivable to that point.
The specific numbers came in February 2024, when the City filed a proof of
claim seeking more than $82 million for breach of contract and negligence:
the buses were contracted to deliver 328 km of range in normal conditions
and 268 km in extreme cold, but averaged about 165 km in winter — 38% below
the cold-weather guarantee — and at best about 250 km in warmer weather,
short of even the 350 km the City had announced at launch. That proof of
claim matches the "$82 million" number that circulates on social media.

Later developments bear directly on the money question. Proterra's transit
business was sold to
Phoenix Motor in January 2024, which said it planned to restock spare parts.
Proterra's reorganization plan took effect in March 2024. As of a May 2026
claims-register entry, Edmonton's claim sits as general unsecured, with no
allowed or paid amount shown publicly. The City's own project page still
lists all 60 electric buses in the ETS fleet, operating from two garages.

The three claims this story checks — that the buses
failed, that Edmonton lost $82 million, and that this proves electric buses
can't work in cold cities — turn out to deserve three different answers.
