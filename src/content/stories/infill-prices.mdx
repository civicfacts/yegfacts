A single sentence does the work in most Edmonton infill arguments: a
$350,000 house comes down and a $1,000,000 house goes up in its place. It
arrives as an objection to the word affordable, and it packs two different
assertions into one breath. The first is about a price relationship on a
particular lot. The second is about whether the housing built there is
affordable to anyone.

The brief frozen before this panel ran split them, gave each its own
proposition, and fixed the measures in advance. Both came back Not
established. That is a statement about Edmonton's public record, not a
finding that the replacement housing is cheap.

### What the record can show

Quite a lot, as long as you do not need the two halves joined. The City
counts the redevelopment. Its 2024 Redeveloping Area Infill Report records
286 new single-detached houses built in the redeveloping area against 387
demolished, a net loss of 101, and says plainly that other built forms are
replacing the detached houses that come down. The 2023 edition counts
demolitions by dwelling type on the same basis, on the order of 390 houses
that year. Neither report carries a price or an assessed value for any of
those properties.

The City also counts what replaced them. Administration's one-year review of
Zoning Bylaw 20001 reports 16,511 dwelling units approved in 2024, a 30
percent increase, 40 percent of them in the redeveloping area, with a large
share of the multi-dwelling permits being eight-unit row housing in the RS
zone. That mix is the reason a per-lot answer and a per-dwelling answer can
point in opposite directions on the same street: eight row houses are worth
more together than the bungalow they replaced, and less each.

### Why matched teardown prices are not published

The failure is a join, not a gap in any one dataset.

- The open general building permit stream loses most house demolitions after
  2018, so the review's declared frame, every residential demolition permit
  from 2016 to 2025, cannot be reconstructed from it. The development permit
  stream starts in 2019 and captures only part of the same frame.
- The historical assessment roll publishes account, assessment year, address,
  legal description, coordinates, year built, lot size and total assessed
  value. It does not publish the land and improvement split, the number of
  dwellings inside a title, or any separately titled flag, which are three of
  the fields the review's valuation rules turn on.
- The two sides of the comparison are different kinds of number. The City
  states that an assessed value is a mass-appraisal estimate of market value
  at a legislated date, not a transaction price. Actual arm's-length sale
  prices are held in Alberta's land-title registry and come out parcel by
  parcel for a fee, never as an open dataset.
- Nobody has published the join. As of August 2026 the City was being asked to
  analyse how infill affects property values, which is the clearest evidence
  that the matched comparison did not exist on this review's freeze date.

The mature-neighbourhood half of the question has its own problem: there is no
boundary in force to draw. Zoning Bylaw 12800, whose section 814.2 applied the
Mature Neighbourhood Overlay to sites zoned RF1 through RF5 inside a mapped
appendix, was repealed on 2024-01-01. What survives is the City's open layer
of the 111 neighbourhoods the Overlay defined, which is a list of
neighbourhoods rather than the parcel-by-parcel zoning test the
<Term t="office consolidation">office consolidation</Term> actually wrote down.

### The one reconstruction, and why it did not settle anything

One reviewer built the matched set itself rather than report that nothing
existed. Working from the assessment rolls instead of the permits, it reached
62.0 percent of 6,603 classified replacement dwelling units assessed above the
dwelling they replaced, at a median ratio of 1.222 in constant dollars. Read
by title rather than by dwelling, the same set runs the other way.

It did not carry the verdict, for a reason the brief had fixed before anyone
saw a result. Roughly 3,000 recent units could not be verified as complete, so
the bounds run from 42.6 to 73.8 percent. A range that straddles 50 percent is
neither Supported nor Contradicted, and the reviewer that produced it said so
itself. The other two seats went further in <Term t="cross-review">cross-review</Term>, documenting that the
reconstruction swaps the permit frame for assessment-detected candidates,
infers which dwellings sit on which title, and reports fractional dwelling
units, which a self-contained dwelling cannot be. The closest published
lot-level work, an independent 2026 analysis of 1,242 lots with a median gross
uplift near $1.7 million per lot on 67 rowhouse lots, measures lot totals on a
different cohort and cannot be converted into the per-dwelling share the
proposition asks for.

The circulating $350,000 to $1,000,000 example was never tested. No address,
no source, no property, and the round numbers were not treated as thresholds.

### The affordability test, and where the panel split

The second claim is a model, and the brief says so in as many words: what
would it cost a household on the City median income to buy one of these
dwellings, at 20 percent down over 25 years, with property tax and a fixed
utility allowance on top. It is not what anyone actually paid.

Every input to that model is published except the one it turns on. Statistics
Canada supplies the 30 percent classification, the Bank of Canada the posted
five-year rate, the City its residential mill rates, and Statistics Canada the
household spending line behind the $377.67 monthly utility allowance. The
income figure, a declared $90,000 median household income that puts the line
at $2,250 a month, was cited to two Census Profile pages that returned a File
not found body when the run archived them. It never reached the evidence
registry.

Run anyway, the model put 88.4 percent of 3,359 separately titled replacement
dwellings at or over the line. One seat called that Supported. The other two
did not, on the ground that the denominator is inferred rather than
established, that every price in it is an assessed value standing in for a
sale price nobody could look up, and that about half the replacement dwellings
in the set sit inside single-title buildings and are outside the test
altogether. The panel is recorded as Split and the finding rests on round one,
where the majority found the record silent.

Two things are worth keeping straight at the end. The City's own affordability
work exists and reaches a different question: on its method, a median-income
household could afford benchmark row and apartment product in 2024 and could
not afford benchmark single-detached. And the City's legal definition of
affordable housing covers subsidised, income-tested units, which market infill
is outside by construction. Neither one answers what was asked here, which is
whether the housing that replaced the houses that came down would clear a 30
percent line. On the public record as it stands, that cannot be answered.
