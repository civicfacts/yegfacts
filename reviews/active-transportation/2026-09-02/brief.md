# Review brief: Active transportation investment

Status: PROVISIONAL, drafted 2026-09-02 by Stew under D-0019; revised the
same day after the intake triage (triage-1.md: GO on Claim A, PARK on
Claim B, overruled in writing in triage-decision.md); goes to the framing
check before freezing. Methodology: v1.13. Fourth story of the
pre-registered launch slate (docs/DESIGN.md section 7).

## Story

Edmonton's bike-lane argument runs on two numbers people state as fact.
Opponents say the City is spending $100 million on bike lanes, and the
figure is usually set beside an annual budget line as if it were an
annual one. The City and supporters say the network reduces traffic
congestion. The slate registered both, in the wording below, on
2026-08-31 before any panel ran. This brief tests each on its own.
Topics: transportation, city-finances.

Claims in this story, reviewed and reported separately:

- `at-100m-a-year`: whether the City of Edmonton spends about
  $100 million a year on bike lanes.
- `at-congestion-reduced`: whether Edmonton's bike lanes have reduced
  motor-vehicle traffic congestion on the streets where they were built.

Each claim carries its own verdict, panel agreement and evidence-basis
label. Reviewer confidence appears only beside the reviewer who gave it.
A finding on one claim is not a finding on the other: how much the City
spends says nothing about what the spending did, and the reverse.

## Why this story (selection rationale)

- Prevalence: "$100 million" and "bike lanes" have travelled together in
  Edmonton since council's budget vote of 2022-12-09, in news headlines,
  a councillor's comparison to road spending, and an advocacy campaign
  page. The congestion effect is asserted by the City in its own project
  page and reports, and the reverse effect is asserted by the Province of
  Alberta ahead of legislation expected in fall 2026. The intake record
  (`intake.md`) holds every captured form with its URL and date.
- Civic importance: the Infrastructure Committee heard on 2026-08-26 a
  recommendation to re-evaluate 14 of the program's remaining routes and
  declined it; the Province has said it will legislate on municipal
  bike-lane authority this fall. Both sides argue from these two claims.
- Verifiability: the City publishes its capital budget by profile and
  by year, program updates to council, and traffic data; the spending
  claim is a matter of published record. The congestion claim turns on
  whether anyone in Edmonton has measured it, which is itself a finding
  worth publishing.

## Circulating forms

The captured forms, with URLs and dates, are in `intake.md`, which
reviewers do not receive. In summary, for the panel:

**Claim A** circulates as "spending $100 million on more bike lanes",
"$100 million of spending, requested by Mayor Sohi", "almost double the
City's annual snow-clearing budget" (an advocacy page), and "Only $100
million is going to bike lanes but $1.8 billion ... is going towards

many words; the slate wording makes explicit the annual reading that
the snow-clearing comparison implies. Both readings are handled below.

**Claim B** circulates as the City's own statements that active
transportation "reduces vehicle trips and traffic congestion" (project
page) and that network improvements "will also help manage traffic
congestion" (capital profile CM-20-0330, 2023; report IS03688,
2026-08-26), and as the reverse assertion from opponents, including the
Province's invitation for complaints where bike lanes are "creating
traffic congestion" (2026-07-15) and reader comments that lanes "congest
traffic".

**Prevalence:** established for the "$100 million" figure by the
captured news, council and advocacy sources; established for the
congestion claim by the City's published wording and the Province's.
Neither claim was captured from an individual social-media post in this
intake.

## Who asks this

A resident or a reporter would ask, in their words:

- Claim A: "Is Edmonton really spending $100 million a year on bike
  lanes? How much is it actually?"
- Claim B: "Have the bike lanes made traffic better or worse?"

Each proposition below answers that question at the level the record
answers it, and the required calculations make the panel report the
actual figures whichever way the verdict falls, so the reader gets the
number and not only a verdict word. For Claim B the intake triage found
that the record does not join the current expansion to citywide travel
times with any basis for attribution, so the proposition is stated at
the level the record does hold, the streets where the lanes were built,
and the citywide reading is a qualification.

## Dates fixed in this brief

**As-of date (freeze date):** 2026-09-02.

**Accounting years for Claim A:** the City's fiscal years, which are
calendar years. The completed years of the 2023-2026 capital cycle are
2023, 2024 and 2025. 2026 is in progress on the as-of date and is
reported on approved budget only.

**Period for Claim B:** the active-transportation network as built on
2026-09-02, with the two identifiable interventions being the Downtown
Bike Network (opened summer 2017) and the routes opened under the Active
Transportation Network Expansion Program (2024 onward). Measurements
from any date up to the as-of date count.

Anything decided or published after 2026-09-02, including provincial
legislation, is out of scope for both claims.

## Claims under review

### Claim A, id: at-100m-a-year

**Normalized proposition:** The City of Edmonton spends about
$100 million a year on bike lanes.

**Why this reading, and the other one.** The circulating figure is true
as a four-year commitment: council approved $100 million in the
2023-2026 capital budget for the Active Transportation Network Expansion
Program. A verdict on that reading would tell a reader nothing the City
does not say on its own web page, so it does not carry the verdict. The
reading people get wrong, and the one the slate registered, is the rate:
whether that money, or anything like it, goes out every year. The
annual reading carries the verdict. The four-year reading is reported,
in words, as the first qualification, so a holder who meant "$100
million over the program" sees that stated plainly and is not told they
were wrong about something they did not say.

**"Bike lanes", fixed here.** Read in the holder's favour, as the widest
fair category: all City of Edmonton spending on cycling and
active-transportation infrastructure, namely protected bike lanes,
on-street bikeways, shared-use pathways built as part of the bike
network, bike parking, bike wayfinding, lighting installed for bike
routes, and the equipment bought to maintain them. It includes the
Active Transportation Network Expansion Program (capital profile
CM-20-0330) in full, and the bike-infrastructure component of any other
capital program where the City publishes that component's cost (for
example a bike route delivered inside a neighbourhood renewal project).
It includes operating spending that the City publishes as specific to
bike infrastructure, such as bike-lane snow clearing, where a figure is
published. It excludes sidewalks built without a bike function, roadway
renewal that happens to carry a painted bike symbol with no published
bike component cost, and the River Valley trail system except where the
City books a segment to the bike network. Where a source gives a
combined active-transportation figure that cannot be split between
cycling and walking, count the whole figure in the holder's favour and
say so.

**"Spends", fixed here.** Money the City actually paid out in the year,
as reported in the City's capital budget performance or progress
reports, financial statements, or program updates to council. Approved
budget is not spending: a profile can be funded and underspent. Where
actual expenditure by year is not published for a component, the
approved budget for that year is used as the ceiling, labelled as such.
All public money the City spends counts, whether raised by the City or
granted by another order of government; report the payer mix where the
record gives it.

**"About $100 million a year", fixed here with thresholds.** The
verdict-bearing figure is the highest single year of bike-lane spending,
as defined, among 2023, 2024 and 2025 (actuals) and 2026 (approved
budget), in nominal Canadian dollars, no inflation adjustment.

- **Supported** if that highest year is at least $75 million, three
  quarters of the claimed figure.
- **Partially supported** if it is more than $50 million and less than
  $75 million: real spending in the tens of millions, short of the claim
  by a margin a holder might call rounding, with the failing part named
  as the magnitude.
- **Contradicted** if it is $50 million or less: the record affirmatively
  shows a rate at half the claim or below.
- **Not established** if the City publishes neither actual expenditure
  nor an approved annual budget from which the highest year can be
  found.

Three quarters is the point at which "about" stops covering the gap in
ordinary speech; half is the point at which the figure is a different
order of claim. Neither cutoff is drawn from an identified pre-existing
standard, so each is stated with its alternative, and results are
required under both: report the verdict the reviewer would reach if the
Supported line were $90 million (nine tenths) and if the Contradicted
line were $40 million. The reviewer reports one verdict, under the
primary cutoffs; the alternatives go in `interpretation_notes` or
`limitations`. No cutoff may be changed after the figures are seen.

**Qualifications reported, never carrying the verdict:**

1. The four-year reading: the total approved for bike lanes over
   2023-2026 and the total actually spent to the latest published date,
   stated in words a reader would use ("$100 million over four years").
2. The average per year over 2023-2026 on approved budget, and over
   2023-2025 on actuals.
3. Per-year spending under the widest and the narrowest fair
   definitions of "bike lanes" (the program alone; the program plus
   every published bike component elsewhere), so the reader can see how
   much the definition moves the figure.

**What this claim does not test.** Whether $100 million is too much or
too little; what roads, transit or snow clearing cost by comparison
(report the comparisons the captured forms make only if a published
figure exists, as context, not as part of the verdict); whether the
program has delivered value; whether the money was well spent.

### Claim B, id: at-congestion-reduced

**Normalized proposition:** Edmonton's bike lanes have reduced
motor-vehicle traffic congestion on the streets where they were built.

**Why this reading.** The City's wording is general ("reduces vehicle
trips and traffic congestion") and prospective ("will also help manage
traffic congestion"). The slate registered the claim as an achieved
outcome for the network, which is how it is used in argument on both
sides: supporters say the lanes have eased traffic, opponents say they
have clogged it. The intake triage found that no published Edmonton
evaluation joins the 2024 to 2027 expansion to citywide travel times
with a basis for attributing a change to the network, and that what the
record does hold is street-level measurement: vehicle volumes and
travel times on particular streets before and after particular routes
opened, and the City's before-and-after monitoring of the Downtown Bike
Network. So the proposition is stated at the street level, the level a
resident who drives one of those streets asks about, and the citywide
reading is the first qualification below. It is a claim about
direction, so the verdict rule states what each direction requires. The
opponents' reverse claim is not a separate proposition: evidence that
the lanes increased congestion on those streets is evidence against
this one, and reviewers say in `interpretation_notes` which of "no
measured change" and "measured increase" a Contradicted rests on.

**"Bike lanes" and "the streets where they were built", fixed here.**
The City of Edmonton's built bicycle and active-transportation network
on 2026-09-02: protected bike lanes, on-street bikeways and shared-use
pathways the City counts in that network, including the Downtown Bike
Network opened in 2017 and the routes opened under the Active
Transportation Network Expansion Program from 2024. A street where a
lane was built is the corridor carrying the route and the parallel or
crossing streets a published evaluation names as affected by it
(diversion). The verdict is carried by street-level measurement. Where
the record holds measurements for some streets and none for most, the
verdict is about the streets measured, and reviewers state how many
routes of the network have any measurement at all, so a reader can see
how far the answer reaches.

**"Traffic congestion", fixed here.** Motor-vehicle delay: travel time,
average speed, or delay relative to free-flow conditions, on a corridor
or citywide, as measured by the City, a peer-reviewed study, a
government or court-commissioned analysis, or a published traffic-data
provider's index with a stated method (for example the TomTom Traffic
Index city series for Edmonton, or Statistics Canada census commute
durations). Vehicle volumes, mode share and cycling counts are not
congestion; they are reported as the qualification below.

**Verdict rule, fixed here.**

- **Supported** requires an Edmonton measurement, as defined, showing
  motor-vehicle delay fell on streets where lanes were built, together
  with an attribution of that fall to the lanes made by the publishing
  body or study, not by the reviewer, and no measured street showing
  the reverse.
- **Contradicted** requires an Edmonton measurement, as defined, showing
  delay rose on streets where lanes were built with an attribution to
  the lanes by the publishing body or study, or the City's own or an
  independent evaluation finding no reduction in delay on the streets
  it measured.
- **Not established** where no Edmonton street-level measurement ties
  a built lane to a change in motor-vehicle delay in either direction,
  including where the only evidence is a citywide index that no
  published analysis attributes to the lanes.
- **Partially supported** is available only if the record shows a
  measured, attributed reduction on some measured streets and a
  measured, attributed increase or no change on others, with the
  failing part named.

**Qualification reported, never carrying the verdict: the citywide
reading.** Report any citywide congestion series for Edmonton over 2017
to 2025 (a traffic-data provider's index, census commute durations, the
City's own network-wide measures), with values by year. A citywide index
moving over years in which Edmonton also grew, rebuilt roads and opened
LRT lines is not attribution. Reviewers report such series as context
and may not treat their direction as a verdict on the lanes. Reviewers
must also look for, and report the presence or absence of, a City
evaluation of the Active Transportation Network Expansion Program
covering traffic effects, and route opening dates joined to
before-and-after traffic data; if neither exists, say so, since that
absence is itself a finding for the story.

**Comparators.** Evidence from other cities on what protected bike lanes
do to motor-vehicle travel times is in scope as transferability evidence
and for limitations, with the statement the methodology requires: why
relevant, how the city differs, what can and cannot be inferred. It
cannot carry Supported or Contradicted on its own, because the
proposition is about an outcome in Edmonton. Reviewers must search for
comparators in both directions and report the strongest of each.

**Second qualification, never carrying the verdict:** the City's
narrower wording, "reduces vehicle trips". Report any Edmonton
measurement of motor-vehicle volumes, mode share or bicycle counts on
network streets before and after installation, with its source and its
limits, as evidence about trips rather than about congestion.

**What this claim does not test.** Whether the lanes are used (a
separate factual question, not on the slate for this story); safety
effects; parking effects; business effects; emergency response times;
whether the Province should legislate; whether any route should keep or
lose parking or a travel lane.

## Stakes

**Claim A.** Supported would mean the opponents' figure is right as a
rate, and the City's "$100 million over four years" understates what
goes out each year; supporters of the program would have to answer for a
much larger spend than they defend. Contradicted would mean the figure
people quote as annual is the whole four-year program and the annual
rate is half of it or less; holders of the claim lose the comparison to
annual budgets they have been making. Partially supported would mean
the rate is in the tens of millions but short of the claim; each side
keeps part of its argument. Not established would mean the City does
not publish bike-lane spending in a form that lets anyone say, which
would itself be news to both sides. Each verdict is reachable on
evidence, and each would change something for one side.

**Claim B.** Supported would give the City's stated benefit a measured
Edmonton basis on the streets measured, and opponents would have to
argue against a measurement rather than an assertion. Contradicted
would mean the City's own evaluations, or an independent one, found no
easing or found delay on the streets they measured, which the City's
project page would then have to answer for. Not established would mean
neither side's congestion claim has an Edmonton street measurement
behind it, which takes the argument away from both; the City, which
asserts the benefit, loses more than the opponents, who assert the
reverse with equally little, and the story would say plainly that the
$100 million program has no published traffic evaluation. Partially
supported would mean the effect differs by street. Each verdict is
reachable and each would surprise someone.

## Scope

Geography: the City of Edmonton. Comparators for Claim B as stated;
none for Claim A beyond context.

Time: as fixed above.

Out of scope: policy questions (whether the program should continue,
whether the Province should legislate, whether the modified approach of
2026-08-26 was wise); ridership as a claim in its own right; safety,
parking, business and emergency-response effects; the value or
cost-effectiveness of the program; provincial actions after the as-of
date.

## Required calculations

- Claim A: a table by year, 2023 to 2026, with approved budget and
  actual expenditure for capital profile CM-20-0330, the source and date
  of each figure, and any adjustment council made to the profile
  (supplemental capital budget adjustments) with its date. The same by
  year for every other published bike-infrastructure component
  included, each named with its source. The highest year under the
  widest and narrowest fair definitions. The four-year total approved
  and the total spent to the latest published date. Averages as
  specified. All in nominal Canadian dollars; state whether a figure is
  in thousands in its source.
- Claim B: for each Edmonton street measurement found, the street, the
  measure (travel time, speed, delay), the before and after periods,
  the values, who measured it and who attributed the change; then the
  count of network routes with any such measurement against the count
  of routes built. For each citywide series, the values by year over
  2017-2025 and a statement that the series is context unless a
  published analysis attributes it. For the second qualification, the
  same for volumes, mode share or counts.

## Reviewer instructions of special note

- Quote operative figures from the City's own documents (capital profile
  sheets, budget adjustment reports, financial statements, program
  updates), and name the document, its report number and its date. A
  news figure is a lead to the document behind it.
- Keep approved from spent. A funded profile is not money out the door.
- Keep the four-year total from the annual rate, and report both.
- Keep intention from outcome. "Will help manage congestion" is a
  policy statement; it establishes what the City intends, never that it
  happened.
- Keep trips from congestion. Fewer vehicles on a corridor is not the
  same measurement as less delay, and the brief treats it as a
  qualification.
- Report one verdict per claim under the primary readings and cutoffs
  fixed here, and every alternative reading's result in
  `interpretation_notes` or `limitations`. The schema carries one
  verdict per claim.
- Record every source URL in full; you have no repo access.
- If a definition, cutoff or date in this brief changes what the honest
  answer is, record a MATERIAL FRAMING CONCERN per prompts/reviewer.md
  and answer the claims as posed alongside it.
