# OpenAI Codex, GPT-5

## 1. Critical: the page repairs one side’s claim before judging it

> “Is the $100 million approved for bike lanes almost double the City's annual snow-clearing budget?”

> “Do roads get $1.8 billion in the 2023-2026 capital budget, about eighteen times the $100 million for bike lanes?”

The first question preserves the opponent’s disputed wording. The second silently corrects the supporter’s recorded “180 times” to eighteen before awarding “Supported.” That conflicts with the claim that both comparisons were tested “in the words each was captured using.” A hostile opponent can reasonably call this unequal treatment.

**Fix:** Apply one rule to both camps. Either judge both quotations as stated, or split each into separate dollar-amount and ratio claims. Put the correction in the verdict: “$1.8 billion is about 18 times $100 million; the quoted 180-times ratio is wrong.”

## 2. Critical: “Partially supported” is the screenshot-ready verdict

> “Partially supported”

> “Part holds; as stated it overreaches.”

The qualification is too vague. A bike-lane opponent can repost the badge as validation that $100 million is “almost double” snow clearing. Yet the page later says the result changes to “Contradicted” with another denominator and that none of the examined figures reaches double.

**Fix:** Make the badge self-contained: “Partially supported using the $64.5M base budget; contradicted using the amended 2022 budget.” Follow immediately with the range, 1.17 to 1.64 times.

## 3. High: the roads verdict is more categorical than its evidence description

> “Supported”

> “The evidence affirmatively establishes it.”

Later, the page says the City publishes no roads-only total, the $1.95 billion figure is constructed under the site’s rule, the official $1.79 billion line includes active pathways and the bike program, and the narrowest definition falls below the site’s threshold. “Affirmatively establishes” suppresses all of that.

**Fix:** Use “Supported under the page’s broad roads definition.” State beside the verdict that the City does not publish a roads-only total.

## 4. High: “roads get” confuses authorization with spending

> “roads get about 19 times as much”

The page later stresses that capital budgets are approvals and says actual road spending remains unknown. “Get” sounds like money spent or delivered. It also presents the site’s constructed $1.95 billion total as an official City figure.

**Fix:** Write: “The adopted 2023-2026 budget authorized about 18 times as much in the City’s combined line, or 19.5 times under this page’s broader profile count.”

## 5. High: the central apples-to-oranges warning is below the main verdicts

> “One side is four years of capital; the other is one year of operating money.”

This is essential to understanding the Facebook claim, but it appears deep in “The snow comparison.” A ten-second reader will see the ratio without seeing that the periods and budget types differ.

**Fix:** Put this sentence directly beneath the first verdict and in the opening summary.

## 6. High: the TL;DR does not begin with the answer

> “Council approved capital profile CM-20-0330 on 2022-12-16, 9 to 4, at $5.95 million, $26.75 million, $33.65 million and $33.65 million…”

This starts with an identifier, a date, a vote and four annual amounts. The Facebook reader needs the plain conclusion first.

**Fix:** Open with: “No single year received $100 million. Council authorized about $100 million across four years. Compared with one annual snow budget, the ratio is 1.17 to 1.64, depending on the City figure used.”

## 7. High: the methodology strip delays the useful explanation

> “Verdicts by a three-model AI panel…”

> “under a published rule…”

> “as of…”

> “last verified…”

> “next review by…”

Six process and date explanations appear between the headline findings and the TL;DR. They compete with the actual answer and likely push it farther down the page.

**Fix:** Retain the verification date and methodology version. Collapse the rest into one “How this verdict was produced” disclosure.

## 8. Medium: the competing denominators are hard to retain

> “the ratio is 1.55”

> “the same calculation gives $85.8 million and a ratio of 1.17”

> “an adjusted budget of $79.5 million… the ratio is 1.26”

> “the ratio is 1.64”

The page gives four snow denominators and several roads definitions across widely separated sections. Readers cannot readily tell which are City-published, reconstructed by the site, current, adjusted or selected for the verdict.

**Fix:** Add a compact comparison table near the top with amount, year, definition, source status and resulting ratio. Mark site-constructed totals explicitly.

## 9. Medium: invented “common forms” invite a straw-man accusation

> “Composite paraphrases written by us…”

> “Paraphrase — not a real post”

A section called “Common forms of the claim” contains statements that nobody is documented as having made and admits it does not measure how common they are. Hostile readers from either camp can say the site invented convenient examples.

**Fix:** Remove the section, use verified examples, or rename it “Illustrative wording” and explain what analytical purpose the invented wording serves.

## 10. Medium: major numbers are separated from their sources

> “By the end of 2025 the City had spent $40.0 million…”

> “administration reported about 35 km of new routes…”

The TL;DR contains consequential numbers without visible inline citations. The reporter must search through “Claims checked” or the long evidence catalogue to connect each number with a record.

**Fix:** Attach a source link or evidence marker to every major number in the TL;DR and narrative.

## 11. Medium: the page claims authority for itself

> “Authority comes from the methodology, not from the models.”

> “so no person chooses the finding”

This reads as institutional self-praise. People still chose the question, thresholds, definitions, exception list and synthesis rule. The categorical wording gives critics an avoidable reason to distrust the process.

**Fix:** Describe the division of responsibility plainly: “Models assess the evidence. A pre-published rule combines their verdicts. Editors define the question, sources and thresholds.”

## 12. Medium: one sentence dismisses legitimate questions outside scope

> “What remains open is narrower than the argument”

This sounds like a judgment that the broader public argument is inflated or unimportant. A reader concerned about safety, congestion, usage or value could read it as taking a side.

**Fix:** State only the boundary: “This page does not assess safety, usage, congestion, cost-effectiveness or whether the program should have been approved.”

## 13. Medium: internal workflow jargon overwhelms residents

> “the brief”

> “cell-plus-additions calculation”

> “all three seats”

> “the holder’s comparison”

> “frozen Supported threshold”

These terms belong in reviewer documentation, not the main story. Tooltips do not cure repeated jargon; they make readers interrupt themselves to decode the prose.

**Fix:** Use “review question,” “base budget plus three additions,” “reviewers,” “source of the claim” and “pre-set cutoff.” Move the remaining process language into the AI-review appendix.

## 14. Low: the same evidence is repeated too many times

The $64.5 million, $85.8 million, $1.79 billion, $1.95 billion and arithmetic-error explanations recur in the headline area, TL;DR, narrative, claim cards, limitations and reviewer notes.

The repetition makes the page feel assembled from model outputs and leaves the reporter unsure which section is canonical.

**Fix:** Keep one concise public explanation, one expandable evidence record and one reviewer appendix. Link between them instead of restating them.

## 15. Low: several lines sound machine-written or need copy-editing

> “The evidence affirmatively establishes it.”

> “the verdict word travels with the name of the budget”

> “Three AI reviewers from different vendors research each claim independently, and blind to each other.”

The first two are stilted; the third is grammatically broken. They draw attention to the machinery rather than the evidence.

**Fix:** Replace them with direct prose: “The evidence supports this claim under the stated definition”; “Always name the snow-budget figure with the verdict”; and “Three reviewers assess each claim independently without seeing one another’s work.”
